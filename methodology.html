Methodology & limitations

Why we test

Drug test strips that are marketed for use in drug checking are currently not regulated by FDA or Canada Health Services, so we have started a lot checking project to make sure that these products are fit for this application.

What we test

  • Can the strip actually detect its target at a realistic concentration? (In the case of benzo and nitazene strips, we test several common target structures.)
  • Does a true negative sample like water sometimes cause false positives?
  • Do other substances that might be present in a drug checking sample cause false positives on the strip?

A note on terms

Each dashboard is organized around three levels: manufacturer (the company that actually makes the physical strip, which is not always disclosed and can change over time even for a familiar brand), brand (the name printed on the box, which is how the strip is sold and marketed), and lot (sometimes called a batch: one specific production run, identified by a lot or batch number printed on the packaging).

One manufacturer can sell strips under several different brand names, and one brand accumulates many different lots over time as new batches are produced. That is why a single brand can show up as many separate entries on a dashboard: each row is a lot, not a brand, and lot-to-lot quality can vary even within the same brand. Testing at the lot level, not just the brand level, is the whole point of this project.

Limitations

  • We are not measuring the limit of detection (LOD) — we are just checking the strips at concentrations that we think are good threshold levels.
  • We are only testing a small number (1–6) of structural variants of the target. For benzos and nitazenes, there are dozens of structural variants that could be present in a sample.
  • Results may be listed as positive (no visible test line) vs negative (any visible test line, even if it is very faint), or we may score line visibility on a 1–10 intensity scale instead, where line intensities of 1–3 are very faint lines that many people will report as positive results. More information is provided in the methodology notes on the dashboards.
  • We are only testing a small number of interferences at concentrations up to 2 mg/mL.

In practice this means if the strip says it's there, it's probably there — but if the strip can't detect the target, there could still be a small amount of the target or a different structural variation. If you prepare samples using a standard 2 mg/mL nominal concentration (e.g., 10 mg scoop + 5 mL water) these results should be reliable.
