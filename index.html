Test Strip Lot Checking Program

Drug test strips help prevent drug overdoses. People can use these strips anywhere, and studies show the results actually change behavior — people report not using, using less, or using in ways that are less likely to cause overdose, like with someone nearby (Goldman et al., 2019). Because they are such a valuable tool, it is critical that they work right.

What we’ve found so far

What we checked

We tested 32 batches of fentanyl strips from 18 companies and 20 batches of xylazine strips from 10 companies. Lab members have sent in 169 test reports so far.

Fentanyl results → Xylazine results →

How they did

Most of the time, the strips worked. In plain water, fentanyl strips were right 99.7% of the time. When fentanyl was there, they found it about 8 in 10 times.

Xylazine strips gave a clear answer in about 7 of 10 tests. About 35% of tests gave a faint line that was too hard to call.

Two limits worth knowing about

  • Faint test lines. At low amounts, some strips show a very faint test lineExample of a very faint test line (see the arrow).. It is easy to miss, especially in poor light. For fentanyl strips we count any visible line as a negative result. For xylazine strips we call a faint line “inconclusive.”

  • False alarms from other drugs. Sometimes a strip says it found the drug when it did not. For fentanyl strips, diphenhydramine (Benadryl) is the most common reason (18 of 32 batches). For xylazine strips, similar medicines like romifidine, tizanidine and clonidine can do it.

Even so, 15 of the 32 fentanyl batches found fentanyl every time and never gave a false alarm in plain water.
